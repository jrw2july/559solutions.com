In 1953, Isaac Asimov wrote an essay called "Social Science Fiction." In it he observed that it is easy to predict an automobile in 1880 and very hard to predict a traffic problem.

Fifteen years later, Frederik Pohl repeated a version of the idea in a magazine editorial, crediting "somebody": a good science fiction story should be able to predict not the automobile but the traffic jam. The line is often attributed to Pohl, but he did not claim it as his own.

That sentence is a useful description of good architecture work. Anyone can describe what a new system will do. The harder job is describing what will happen once it works.

## Speculative stories as consequence maps

The best speculative fiction rarely spends long explaining how the invention works. It spends its time on what life looks like after the invention has become ordinary.

E. M. Forster's story "The Machine Stops," published in 1909, imagines people living alone in underground rooms while a vast Machine meets every need. Over generations, people stop understanding how the Machine works. A system meant to repair it fails, and people dismiss the warning signs because they believe the Machine cannot fail. When it does break down, nobody knows how to fix it.

Read as a story about technology, it is unsettling. Read as a story about systems, it is familiar. A tool that works so well that nobody learns how it works. A maintenance function nobody watches. Warnings explained away because the system has always been fine.

The story works as a map. It starts from a benefit and follows the consequences outward until they reach the people.

## First-order benefits and second-order effects

Every technology decision has a first-order effect: the thing it is supposed to do. A new scheduling system books appointments. An automated approval moves requests faster.

Second-order effects come from people and other systems adjusting to the first change:

- Booking gets easier, so more people book, and no-show rates change.
- Approvals get faster, so reviewers spend less time on each one, and errors that a slower process caught now slip through.
- A report becomes available on demand, so it becomes the number everyone manages to, whether or not it measures what matters.

Second-order effects are not always bad. Some are the best part of a change. The problem is not that they exist. It is that nobody looked for them.

A simple exercise: for any proposed change, write the first-order effect, then ask "and then what?" three times. You will not predict everything. You will predict more than you would have.

## Incentives, power, and uneven impact

Science fiction is also good at asking who wins and who pays. The same technology lands very differently on different people.

In systems work, the same questions apply:

- **Who benefits most?** Often the people who chose the system.
- **Who carries the extra work?** Often people who were not in the room: front-line staff, customers, another department.
- **What behavior does the system reward?** When a measure becomes a target, people tend to work toward the measure, sometimes at the expense of the goal behind it.
- **Who can change it, and who just has to live with it?**

Asking these early is not a political exercise. It is risk management. The people who carry the cost of a design are often the first to find its flaws, and the last to be asked.

## What the system makes easy or difficult

Every system shapes behavior by what it makes easy. A form with a default answer gets that answer more often. A process with ten steps to report a problem gets fewer reports. A tool that hides its reasoning gets questioned less.

Ask of any design:

- What will people do more of, because it is now easy?
- What will people do less of, because it is now harder?
- What will they stop knowing how to do, because the system does it for them?

That last question is Forster's. It is worth asking about automation, AI assistants, and any tool that takes over a skill. Sometimes losing the skill is fine. Sometimes it is the skill you need on the day the system stops.

## Questions to bring back to real work

You do not need to write fiction to use its habits. Before a significant change, try a short consequences session with a few people from different parts of the work:

1. **Fast forward.** It is two years from now and the system works exactly as planned. Describe an ordinary day.
2. **And then what?** List three second-order effects, good or bad.
3. **Who carries it?** For each effect, name who benefits and who does extra work.
4. **What gets easy, what gets hard?** List one behavior that will increase and one that will fade.
5. **The day it stops.** If the system failed for a week, what would people no longer know how to do?

Write the answers down next to the design. Some will turn into requirements. Some will turn into monitoring. A few will change the design itself.

The automobile was always going to get built. The traffic jam was the part worth thinking about in advance.
