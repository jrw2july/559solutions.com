Every small business has at least one. The month-end close that only the office manager knows how to finish. The vendor order that only the owner places, because only the owner remembers which supplier gives the better price in winter. The website update that only one person can do, because only that person knows the password and the order of the steps.

Nothing is wrong, exactly. The work gets done, often very well. The risk is invisible until the day that person is sick, on vacation, or gone.

When a process works only because one person remembers everything, that person's memory has become part of your infrastructure. It deserves the same care as any other critical system.

## Signals that memory has become infrastructure

You may have this problem if:

- **Work waits for one person.** Tasks pile up when they are out, even simple ones.
- **The answer to "how do we do this?" is a name.** "Ask Linda."
- **Vacations are planned around the work.** Someone cannot take time off at month-end, at renewal time, or during the busy season.
- **Mistakes happen when someone else tries.** The steps looked simple, but something was missed that the regular person always knew to do.
- **Nobody else has the access.** Logins, keys, vendor contacts, or approvals sit with one person.

One or two of these is normal. Several of them, around the same piece of work, is a single point of failure.

## Capture triggers, decisions, exceptions, and handoffs

A list of steps is a start, but it is rarely what goes wrong. When a second person tries to run the process, they usually stumble on four other things. Capture them.

**Triggers.** What starts the work? A date, an email, a low stock level, a customer request? If the trigger lives in someone's head ("I just know it's about time"), write it down and, if possible, put it on a calendar or a checklist.

**Decisions.** Where does the person choose between options? Which supplier, which price, whether to approve. Write what they look at and how they usually decide.

**Exceptions.** What happens when something is unusual? A missing invoice, a late delivery, a customer who pays in two parts. Ask: "What went wrong last time, and what did you do?" Exceptions are where most of the hidden knowledge lives.

**Handoffs.** Where does the work pass to someone else, or come back? Who needs to know it is done? A missed handoff can look like a finished process until someone downstream notices.

The easiest way to capture all four is to sit with the person the next time they do the work. Watch, ask questions, and take notes. A screen recording with them talking through it can work too.

## Separate expert judgment from repeatable steps

Not every part of the work can or should be turned into steps. Some of it is real expertise, built over years. Trying to write it all down can feel insulting to the expert, and it often fails anyway.

Sort the work into two groups:

- **Repeatable steps.** Things anyone could do with clear instructions: log in here, download this report, enter these numbers, send it to this address.
- **Judgment calls.** Places where experience matters: spotting an invoice that looks wrong, knowing when a customer needs a phone call instead of an email.

Write the repeatable steps in full. For the judgment calls, write down what the expert looks at and a few examples of what they decided and why. That does not replace the expert. It gives a backup person enough to make a reasonable call, or to know when to wait.

This also frees the expert. When the routine steps can be done by someone else, the expert spends more time on the parts that need them.

## Test the process with a second person

A process is not documented until someone else has run it from the notes.

Pick a low-risk moment. Have a second person do the work while the expert watches and stays quiet. Each time the second person gets stuck, guesses, or asks a question, that is a gap in the notes. Fix the notes, not the person.

Two or three rounds are usually enough to make a process reliable. Checklists have a strong record in high-stakes work: a 2009 study of a surgical safety checklist across eight hospitals, published in the New England Journal of Medicine, found significant drops in deaths and complications after it was introduced. Your month-end close is lower stakes, but the principle is the same. Experienced people miss steps too, and a short list catches them.

## Improve the smallest risky handoff first

You do not need to document everything at once. Start where a missed step would hurt most and the fix is smallest.

Ask two questions about each process that depends on one person:

1. If this were missed or done wrong, how bad would it be?
2. How much effort would it take to make it runnable by someone else?

Start with the work that scores high on the first question and low on the second. Often that is something small: sharing a login through a proper password manager, putting a renewal date on a shared calendar, or writing down the one exception that always causes trouble.

One process a month is a good pace. In a year, the business is much harder to break.

## Keep the person, lose the risk

The goal is not to make anyone replaceable. People who carry this knowledge are usually some of the most committed people in the business. The goal is to make sure the business keeps working on the days they are not there, and to let them take a real vacation.

That is good for the business, and it is kind to the person.
