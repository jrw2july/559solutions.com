Discovery meetings have a reputation problem. People leave them with pages of notes and no clearer idea of what happens next. A week later, someone asks, "Didn't we already talk about this?"

The meeting was not the problem. The shape of it was.

A good discovery meeting does one job: it moves a decision forward. It does not have to finish the decision. It has to leave everyone knowing what was learned, who owns what, and what is still unknown.

Here are five habits that help.

## 1. Publish the decision and the scope before the meeting

Send a short note a day or two ahead. Three lines are enough:

- **The decision we are working toward.** "Whether to replace the intake spreadsheet, and with what kind of tool."
- **What is in scope.** "New client intake, from first contact to the first scheduled call."
- **What is out of scope.** "Billing and contract terms. Those come later."

This does two things. People arrive ready to talk about the same thing. And the people who should be in the room can tell you they are missing before the meeting, not after.

If you cannot write the decision in one sentence, that is the first finding. Spend the meeting getting there.

## 2. Ask about the current work, not the requested feature

Requests usually arrive as solutions: "We need a dashboard." "We need it to send a text." The request matters, but it is a guess about the fix.

Ask about the work as it happens today:

- Walk me through the last time this happened.
- What starts it? What do you do first?
- Where do you wait? Where do you copy something from one place to another?
- What do you do when something is missing or wrong?
- Who else touches it before it is done?

"The last time" is the key phrase. A real example is usually more reliable than a general description, because a general description tends to drift toward how the process is supposed to work. The exceptions and workarounds show up in the stories.

If you can, watch the work happen, or have someone share their screen and do it. Ten minutes of watching often beats an hour of describing.

## 3. Make assumptions and disagreements visible

Every discovery meeting carries assumptions. Some are shared. Some are not, and nobody knows it yet.

Keep a short list on the screen, labeled plainly:

- **We believe:** statements the group treats as true but has not checked. "Most clients come in through the web form."
- **We disagree about:** places where two people gave different answers. "Sales says follow-up happens in a day. Operations says three."

Do not try to settle every item in the room. The point is to see them. An assumption written down can be checked. An assumption that stays in someone's head becomes a surprise later.

Disagreement is useful data. When two people describe the same process differently, you have often found the place where work falls through the cracks.

## 4. Record owners and unanswered questions

Before the meeting ends, turn the notes into two short lists.

**Actions, each with one owner and a date.** "Maria pulls last month's intake numbers by Friday." An action owned by "the team" usually belongs to nobody.

**Open questions, each with someone who will find the answer.** "Do we need to keep intake records for a set number of years? Owner: Dev, check with the accountant."

Read both lists aloud. It takes two minutes, and it is the moment people correct a misunderstanding while everyone is still in the room.

Send the lists within a day, along with the assumptions and disagreements. Keep it short enough that people will read it.

## 5. End with the next smallest decision

Big decisions rarely happen in one meeting. That is fine, as long as the meeting ends with a smaller decision that moves things forward.

Examples:

- "We will map the current intake steps on one page before we look at any tools."
- "We will test whether the form is the real bottleneck by counting how long each step takes for one week."
- "We agree the scope is intake only. Billing waits."

The next decision should be small enough to make today and specific enough that everyone can tell when it is done.

## A simple agenda

For a one-hour discovery meeting:

1. **Five minutes:** restate the decision and the scope.
2. **Thirty minutes:** walk through real, recent examples of the current work.
3. **Ten minutes:** review the assumptions and disagreements list.
4. **Ten minutes:** agree on actions, owners, and open questions.
5. **Five minutes:** name the next smallest decision.

The times are a starting point. The order matters more than the minutes.

## The measure of a good discovery meeting

A useful test, the day after:

> Could someone who missed the meeting read the notes and know what was decided, who owns what, and what we still do not know?

If yes, the meeting did its job. If not, the next one should start with those three things.
