Many organizations have a process document that nobody can actually follow. It exists. It was reviewed. It may even have a version number. And when the person who usually does the work is away, the process still stops.

The document was not wrong. It was incomplete in a specific way: it described the steps, but not what the person running the steps knew.

That gap is why good processes fail.

## A process can look complete while the exceptions stay invisible

A process document usually describes the normal path. Receive the request, check the details, approve it, notify the customer. Four steps, clean and correct.

Real work is mostly the abnormal path:

- The request arrives with a field missing.
- The customer is on an old contract with different terms.
- The system that should send the notice is down.
- Two people approved the same request.

An experienced person handles these without thinking. They do not consider them part of "the process," so they do not mention them when the process is written down. The document looks complete because the exceptions never made it to the page.

One practical test: count how many times in the last month the work did not follow the normal path. If the number is high and the document covers none of those cases, the document describes the process the organization hopes it has.

## Capture judgment, not only steps

The philosopher Michael Polanyi described this problem in 1966: "we can know more than we can tell." His point was that much of what skilled people know is tacit. Riding a bicycle is the classic example. You can do it, but you cannot fully explain how.

Process knowledge works the same way. An experienced person "just knows" an order looks suspicious, or a customer needs a call instead of an email. Asking them to write the process down will get you the steps. It will not get you the judgment.

Some ways to get closer:

- **Ask for stories, not rules.** "Tell me about a time this went wrong." Stories carry the judgment that rules leave out.
- **Ask what they look at.** "When you check a request, what is the first thing your eye goes to?"
- **Collect real examples.** Five past cases, each with what the person decided and why, teach more than a page of general rules.
- **Watch the work.** Sit with them while they do it. Notice the pauses. A pause is often a judgment call.

You will not capture all of it. You can capture enough for someone else to make a reasonable decision, or to recognize when a case needs to wait for an expert.

## Make ownership and escalation explicit

Processes often fail at the edges, where it is unclear who decides.

For each process, write down:

- **The owner.** One person or role who answers for whether it works, not just who does the steps today.
- **The backup.** Who runs it when the usual person is away, and have they actually done it?
- **The escalation path.** When something does not fit the normal path, who should the person ask, and how quickly do they need an answer?
- **The authority.** What can the person running the process decide alone, and what needs approval?

Without this, people who are not the regular operator often freeze. They are not sure they are allowed to make a call, so they wait. Waiting is how a small exception becomes a late payment or an unhappy customer.

## Rehearse absence before it becomes a crisis

The real test of a process is the day the expert is not there. Most organizations find out how ready they are at the worst possible moment.

Find out on purpose instead. Pick a normal week. Ask the backup to run the process while the expert stays available but hands-off. Keep notes on every question, delay, and guess.

That rehearsal will show you exactly what the document is missing. It also builds the backup's confidence, which matters as much as the notes. A backup who has done the work once is very different from one who has only read about it.

Rotate who serves as backup. Repeat the rehearsal every few months, and after any significant change to the process.

## Treat documentation as an operating tool

Documentation fails when it is treated as a record, written once and filed away. It works when it is part of how the work gets done.

A few habits help:

- **Keep it where the work happens.** A checklist next to the task, a link in the system, notes in the shared folder people already use.
- **Update it every time something new happens.** When an exception comes up that is not covered, the person who handled it adds a line. Small, frequent changes keep it alive.
- **Use it, even when you know the steps.** If the expert follows the checklist too, gaps get noticed and fixed.
- **Date it and name an owner.** Readers need to know whether it is current, and who to ask if it is not.

A process document that is used every week stays accurate. One that is used only in an emergency is usually out of date when the emergency arrives.

## The real goal

A good process is not one that is well written. It is one that a second person can run on an ordinary Tuesday without calling anyone.

> If the person who usually does this were unavailable for two weeks, what would stop?

Whatever is on that list is where to start.
